SECTION 1: INTRODUCTION TO DARK MATTER FOUNDATIONS

The concept of dark matter emerged from a fundamental discrepancy in astronomical observations. When studying the rotation of galaxies, astronomers noticed that the outer regions were moving much faster than could be explained by the visible matter alone. This observation forms our first crucial understanding:

The Rotation Curve Problem:
When we observe spiral galaxies, we can measure both:
1. The distribution of visible matter (stars, gas, dust)
2. The velocity of stars at different distances from the galactic center

According to Newtonian dynamics, these velocities should follow a specific pattern. For a star at radius r from the center:

$$ v(r) = \sqrt{\frac{GM(r)}{r}} $$

Where:
- v(r) is the orbital velocity at radius r
- G is the gravitational constant
- M(r) is the mass enclosed within radius r

Let's understand why this equation is important:
1. If most mass is concentrated in the visible disk, M(r) should stop increasing significantly beyond the visible edge
2. Therefore, v(r) should decrease as 1/√r beyond the visible galaxy
3. However, observations show that v(r) remains nearly constant

This "flat rotation curve" tells us something profound - there must be additional mass we cannot see. This invisible mass:
- Extends far beyond the visible galaxy
- Forms a roughly spherical "halo"
- Contains most of the galaxy's total mass

To quantify this, let's examine the mass distribution:
For a constant velocity v in the outer regions:

$$ M(r) = \frac{rv^2}{G} $$

This means the mass must increase linearly with radius, even where we see no additional visible matter.

Understanding this discrepancy requires careful consideration of:
1. Observational techniques and their limitations
2. Alternative explanations (modified gravity theories)
3. Implications for galaxy formation and evolution
